Health Insurance for Truck Drivers & Owner-Operators

Health insurance for owner-operators and leased drivers who cross state lines every week: national PPO networks, deductible premiums and coverage that works on the road.

Reviewed by Ayoub Cherif, licensed health insurance broker (NPN 22089749) · Updated November 17, 2026

Get a Free Quote Call Us Now

Over-the-road drivers need a plan that works in the state where they get sick, not just the state on their CDL. For healthy owner-operators above the subsidy line, a private PPO with a national network is usually the best fit, and premiums are generally deductible on IRS Form 7206.

What owner-operators and company drivers actually need

A company driver usually has an employer plan; the questions start when you lease onto a carrier or get your own authority, because then coverage is your job. Three things decide whether a plan works for a driver: whether routine and urgent care are in network in the states you actually run, whether the deductible fits a business with uneven months, and whether you can keep a primary care doctor at home who knows your history for DOT physicals.

Regional HMO and EPO plans usually cover only emergencies outside their home state, which is why most of our healthy owner-operators choose a PPO with a national network. Because the plan is yours, it doesn't change when you switch carriers or move from leased to your own authority. If you have a net profit, premiums are generally deductible on IRS Form 7206.

What matters for truck drivers and owner-operators

  • Coverage on the road: urgent care and doctors in network in other states, not just emergencies.
  • DOT physicals: your medical certificate is valid for up to 24 months; keep a primary care doctor who knows your history.
  • Leased vs. owner-operator: leased drivers paid on 1099 are generally self-employed for tax purposes; company drivers on W-2 usually are not.

Between jobs or retiring early?

A gap in coverage is where people overpay most, usually on COBRA. For healthy people a private PPO usually costs less and can start any time. See health insurance between jobs.

The honest caveat: private PPO plans are medically underwritten. They can limit or exclude pre-existing conditions and approval isn't guaranteed. If someone in your household has a serious ongoing condition, a Marketplace plan can be the better choice because it can't turn you down, and we'll tell you if that's you.

Alabama: From the Port of Mobile to the Tennessee line

Containers leave the Port of Mobile on I-65 and I-10, and Birmingham sits where I-65 meets I-20/59. Huntsville's manufacturers ship north into Tennessee, so an Alabama driver can cross three states before lunch. An Alabama plan built around one metro won't follow you.

List the states you ran last year and check the plan's network in each, not just Alabama. Keep a primary care doctor in Birmingham, Huntsville or Mobile for blood pressure and sleep checks that affect your medical card. Alabama has no general small-employer continuation law, so if you leave a small carrier, coverage can end the day you go.

Arkansas: Northwest Arkansas and the I-40 corridor

Some of the country's largest trucking and retail companies are based in Northwest Arkansas, and I-40, I-30 and I-49 carry their freight in every direction. Many Arkansas drivers are leased to a national carrier and spend most nights out of state.

If you're leased to a large carrier, ask what happens to any carrier-sponsored plan the day you leave, then compare it with a plan you own. Check UAMS or Washington Regional at home and urgent care in the states you run most. Arkansas's continuation law is short: 120 days for small-employer plans.

More on Arkansas: health insurance in Arkansas

Colorado: I-70 over the mountains and I-25 along the Front Range

I-25 runs the Front Range from Fort Collins to Pueblo, and I-70 crosses the Rockies through the Eisenhower Tunnel, where commercial vehicles must carry chains through the winter season. Long stretches between towns mean the nearest in-network clinic matters more than it does in a city.

Check urgent care along I-70 and I-25, the hospitals you'd use in Denver or Grand Junction and the states at the other end of your runs. If you leave a small Colorado carrier, the state's continuation law can keep that plan for up to 18 months at full price, but a private PPO is usually cheaper if you're healthy.

More on Colorado: health insurance in Colorado

Delaware: I-95 and the Port of Wilmington

Delaware is small, but I-95 carries East Coast freight straight through Wilmington, and the Port of Wilmington handles fruit, cars and containers. A Delaware driver's day often starts in Delaware and ends in Pennsylvania, New Jersey or Maryland.

Make sure the plan covers Pennsylvania, New Jersey and Maryland providers, since most Delaware runs end there. Keep a ChristianaCare or Bayhealth primary care doctor for DOT-related checkups. Delaware's continuation law allows nine months on a small-employer plan.

More on Delaware: health insurance in Delaware

Georgia: The Port of Savannah and Atlanta's interstates

The Port of Savannah is one of the country's busiest container ports, and I-16 feeds its boxes to I-75 and I-95. Atlanta sits where I-75, I-85 and I-20 meet, so Georgia drivers move freight to Florida, the Carolinas and the Midwest every day.

Check urgent care and hospitals along I-75, I-85 and I-95 in Florida, the Carolinas and Tennessee, plus your home system in Atlanta or Savannah. Georgia Access plans are often regional, so the out-of-state network is the deciding factor for most drivers.

More on Georgia: health insurance in Georgia

Illinois: Chicago, the country's rail and freight hub

Chicago is the busiest rail and intermodal hub in North America, and drivers move containers between rail yards, warehouses in Joliet and Elwood, and I-80, I-55 and I-57. Downstate drivers haul grain and farm freight to river terminals.

Chicago-area drivers should check Indiana and Wisconsin providers; downstate drivers, Missouri and Iowa. Avoid Chicago-only HMOs if you ever take regional or long-haul loads. Illinois's continuation law keeps a small-employer plan for 12 months if you leave a small carrier.

More on Illinois: health insurance in Illinois

Indiana: The Crossroads of America

I-65, I-69, I-70 and I-74 meet around Indianapolis, and more interstates cross Indiana than almost any other state. Northwest Indiana drivers work the Chicago market, and Fort Wayne and Elkhart ship auto parts and RVs across the Midwest.

Check IU Health, Community or Parkview at home and urgent care in Illinois, Ohio, Michigan and Kentucky. Indiana has no general small-employer continuation law, so drivers leaving a small fleet should line up their own plan before the last day.

More on Indiana: health insurance in Indiana

Iowa: I-80, I-35 and the farm economy

I-80 crosses Iowa east to west and I-35 runs north to south through Des Moines. Near Walcott, Iowa 80 bills itself as the world's largest truck stop. Iowa drivers haul grain, livestock, ethanol and manufactured goods to every neighboring state.

Check that the plan covers the neighboring states you deliver to, and that a primary care doctor near home is in network for medical-card follow-ups. Iowa's continuation law allows nine months on a small-employer plan.

More on Iowa: health insurance in Iowa

Kansas: I-70, I-35 and the Kansas City logistics hub

BNSF's intermodal logistics park in Edgerton anchors the Kansas side of the Kansas City market, and I-35 and I-70 cross there. Out west, drivers haul grain and cattle along I-70 and US-54 to Colorado, Oklahoma and Texas.

Check both sides of the Kansas City metro, plus Colorado, Oklahoma and Texas if you run west or south. Kansas's continuation law allows up to 18 months on a small-employer plan.

More on Kansas: health insurance in Kansas

Kentucky: Louisville, Northern Kentucky and the I-75 corridor

UPS's Worldport air hub is in Louisville and Amazon's air hub is near the Cincinnati airport in Hebron, so freight moves around the clock. I-75 carries auto parts between Kentucky's plants and the Great Lakes, and I-65 links Louisville to Nashville.

Check Ohio, Indiana, Tennessee and Michigan providers if you run I-75 or I-65, plus UK HealthCare or Norton at home. Kentucky's continuation law allows up to 18 months on a small-employer plan. kynect plans are often regional.

More on Kentucky: health insurance in Kentucky

Louisiana: I-10, the river and the petrochemical corridor

I-10 runs from Lake Charles through Baton Rouge to New Orleans, alongside refineries and chemical plants on the Mississippi. The Port of New Orleans and the river terminals keep drayage and tanker drivers busy, and I-20 and I-49 carry freight north.

Texas coverage is the most important check for western Louisiana drivers, and Mississippi and Alabama for drivers on I-10 and I-12 east. Louisiana's continuation law allows 12 months on a small-employer plan.

Maryland: The Port of Baltimore and the I-95 corridor

The Port of Baltimore handles more cars and light trucks than any other U.S. port, and I-95 and I-70 carry freight to the rest of the East Coast. Western Maryland drivers use I-81 and I-68 toward Pennsylvania and West Virginia.

Check Pennsylvania, Virginia, Delaware and West Virginia providers, plus your home system. Maryland's continuation law allows up to 18 months on a small-employer plan; Maryland Health Connection plans vary in out-of-state coverage.

More on Maryland: health insurance in Maryland

Michigan: Auto plants and the Canada border

The Ambassador Bridge between Detroit and Windsor is one of the busiest commercial crossings on the U.S.–Canada border, and Michigan's auto plants depend on parts moving on I-94, I-75 and I-96 every day. Many Michigan drivers cross into Ohio and Indiana daily.

Check Ohio and Indiana providers and the plan's out-of-country terms if you cross into Canada. Michigan has no general small-employer continuation law, so coverage from a small fleet can end the day you leave.

More on Michigan: health insurance in Michigan

Mississippi: I-55, I-20 and the Gulf Coast

I-55 runs from New Orleans through Jackson to Memphis, and I-20 crosses the state from Louisiana to Alabama. The Port of Gulfport and the Gulf Coast shipyards add drayage, and DeSoto County sits in the Memphis distribution market.

Check Tennessee, Louisiana and Alabama providers, plus UMMC or Baptist at home. Mississippi's continuation law allows 12 months on a small-employer plan.

More on Mississippi: health insurance in Mississippi

Missouri: Springfield, Kansas City and St. Louis

Springfield is home to one of the largest trucking companies in the country, and I-44 and I-70 make Missouri a crossroads between Texas, the Midwest and the East. Kansas City and St. Louis both sit on state lines.

If you're leased to a Springfield carrier, compare any carrier-sponsored plan with a plan you own. Check Kansas and Illinois providers near the metros. Missouri's continuation law runs the same length as federal COBRA for small employers.

More on Missouri: health insurance in Missouri

Montana: I-90, I-94 and long empty miles

I-90 crosses Montana from Idaho to Wyoming, I-94 heads east to North Dakota and I-15 runs to Canada. Grain, cattle and energy freight cover huge distances, and towns with a hospital can be more than an hour apart.

Check small-town clinics along I-90, I-94 and I-15 and the states you deliver to. Montana has no general small-employer continuation law, so line up your own plan before leaving a small outfit.

Nebraska: Omaha and the I-80 corridor

Omaha is home to major trucking companies, and I-80 carries freight the full length of Nebraska between Chicago and Denver. Grand Island, Kearney and North Platte are the stops in between, and North Platte's Bailey Yard is the world's largest rail yard.

Check Iowa, Wyoming, Colorado and Utah providers along I-80, plus Nebraska Medicine or Bryan Health at home. Nebraska's continuation law allows six months after an involuntary job loss on a small-employer plan.

Nevada: Reno's warehouses and the Las Vegas corridor

The Tahoe Reno Industrial Center east of Reno is one of the largest industrial parks in the country, and I-80 carries its freight to California and Utah. In the south, I-15 links Las Vegas to Southern California and Utah.

Check California and Utah providers, since nearly every Nevada run crosses one of those lines. Nevada has no general small-employer continuation law, and Nevada Health Link plans are often regional.

North Carolina: Charlotte, the Triad and I-95

Charlotte's distribution centers sit where I-85 meets I-77, the Triad around Greensboro is crossed by I-40 and I-85, and I-95 carries East Coast freight through the eastern half of the state. The Port of Wilmington adds container traffic.

Check Georgia, South Carolina and Virginia providers along I-85, I-95 and I-77, plus your home system. North Carolina's continuation law allows up to 18 months on a small-employer plan.

Ohio sits within a day's drive of much of the U.S. population, and Columbus's Rickenbacker logistics hub and the I-70, I-71 and I-75 corridors keep owner-operators busy. I-80 across the north links Ohio to Pennsylvania and Indiana.

Check Indiana, Pennsylvania, Kentucky and Michigan providers along I-70, I-71, I-75 and I-80, plus Cleveland Clinic or OhioHealth at home. Ohio's continuation law allows up to 12 months on a small-employer plan.

Check Texas, Arkansas and Kansas providers along I-35, I-40 and I-44, plus OU Health or Saint Francis at home. Oklahoma's continuation rule for small employers is short: 63 days, or six months for longer-covered employees.

The Port of Charleston ships containers up I-26 to the inland port in Greer, near the Upstate's auto and manufacturing plants on I-85. I-95 carries East Coast freight through the eastern side of the state.

Memphis is one of the country's busiest freight hubs, with a giant air-cargo hub, river ports and the I-40 and I-55 corridors, while Nashville sits where I-40, I-24 and I-65 meet. Tennessee borders eight states, tied with Missouri for the most of any state.

FAQ

Truck Drivers & Owner-Operators: questions

What is the best health insurance for owner-operators?

For most healthy owner-operators, a private PPO with a national network, so care on the road is in network. Premiums are generally deductible on Form 7206.

Can truck drivers deduct health insurance?

Self-employed drivers with a net profit can generally deduct premiums for themselves, a spouse and dependents on IRS Form 7206. Company drivers on W-2 generally cannot.

Will a health plan from my home state cover me on the road?

Only if its network reaches the states you run. A PPO with a national network covers routine and urgent care across the U.S.; many regional plans cover only emergencies out of state.

Do I need a special plan for my DOT physical?

No. The physical is done by a certified medical examiner. A primary care doctor in network at home helps you manage conditions like blood pressure that can affect your medical card.
