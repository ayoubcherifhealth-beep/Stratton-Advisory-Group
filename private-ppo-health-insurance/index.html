Private PPO Health Insurance for Individuals and Families

What a private, medically underwritten PPO is, who it fits, and why healthy people above the subsidy line usually get more for their money with one.

Reviewed by Ayoub Cherif, licensed health insurance broker (NPN 22089749) · Updated November 21, 2026

Book a Free Consultation Call Us Now

A private PPO is individual health insurance that a carrier prices on your age, location and health instead of your income. For healthy people who earn too much for a premium tax credit, it's usually the better value: many plans use national PPO networks, you can see specialists without referrals, and you can apply year-round.

Why private PPO plans can cost less for healthy people

Marketplace plans are guaranteed issue: everyone is accepted, healthy or not, and everyone shares one pool. Prices are set from the expected cost of that whole pool, so healthy people help pay for the care of people with serious conditions. Your health can’t lower your price.

Private PPO plans work differently. You answer health questions and the carrier approves you first. If you’re approved, you’re priced with other healthy, lower-risk people who are less likely to need expensive care. That’s why healthy individuals and families often get a lower rate, frequently with a national PPO network.

Qualifying for a tax credit doesn’t always make a Marketplace plan the better deal. The credit lowers your premium, not your deductible or out-of-pocket maximum, so you can still pay a lot before your insurance really starts helping.

The trade-off: a private plan can decline you or limit coverage for a pre-existing condition. If that happens, we’ll compare Marketplace options with you instead.

Who a private PPO fits best

  • Healthy adults and families above the 2027 subsidy line: $63,840 for one person, $86,560 for two, $132,000 for a family of four.
  • Self-employed people and business owners (premiums generally deductible on IRS Form 7206).
  • People who travel for work or split time between states and want in-network care wherever they are.
  • People leaving a job who were offered COBRA, or who feel they're overpaying for a work plan.

We work with adults ages 18 to 64 and their families.

Worth knowing: Private plans aren't guaranteed: they ask health questions first. A pre-existing condition can be excluded or lead to a decline. When a Marketplace plan is the better answer for your household, we'll say so.

What sets the price of a private PPO

A private PPO is priced on you, not on your income. The carrier looks at:

  • Age and ZIP code. The same plan costs more at 55 than at 35, and more in some counties than others.
  • Your answers to the health questions. Healthy applicants get the best rates. Some conditions raise the price, are excluded, or lead to a decline.
  • Tobacco use. Smokers pay more.
  • The plan design you choose. A higher deductible lowers the monthly premium; a lower out-of-pocket maximum raises it.
  • Who is on the plan. Each family member is rated, so a household's price depends on everyone's age and health.

Because income plays no part, a private PPO is often the better value once you earn too much for a premium tax credit, which for 2027 coverage generally means household income above 400% of the federal poverty level.

What a private PPO covers, and what to read closely

Not every “PPO” is full comprehensive coverage. Some plans sold as PPOs are limited-benefit or fixed-indemnity plans that pay set amounts. We show you which is which before you decide.

The private PPO plans we compare are full comprehensive coverage: doctor visits, specialists, hospital stays, surgery, emergencies and prescriptions. They are not built the same way as Marketplace plans, so a few details deserve a careful read:

  • Pre-existing conditions. A condition you disclose can be excluded, limited for a waiting period, or lead to a decline. Read the approval letter for any exclusion rider.
  • Benefits a Marketplace plan must include. Some private plans limit or leave out benefits such as maternity care. If you plan to grow your family, say so before you apply.
  • Prescriptions. Check that your regular medications are on the plan's drug list and which tier they fall in.
  • The network name. "PPO" describes how the plan works, not which doctors are in it. Ask which national network the plan uses and search your doctors and hospital in that network.

Surprise medical bills: which plans are protected

The federal No Surprises Act stops most surprise bills from out-of-network providers for emergency care, for certain care at in-network hospitals and surgery centers, and for air ambulances. It protects people with employer group plans and with individual health insurance bought on or off the Marketplace.

It does not cover everything sold as "health coverage." Short-term plans, health care sharing ministries, fixed indemnity plans and farm bureau plans fall outside these billing protections, and ground ambulance bills aren't covered by the law at all. Before you buy, ask whether the plan is individual or group health insurance. We tell you which kind every plan we show you is.

Checklist before you buy a private PPO

  1. Who issues the plan? Get the insurance company's name, not just the brand or network.
  2. Is it health insurance? Confirm it is individual or group health insurance, not a short-term plan, sharing ministry or fixed indemnity plan.
  3. Which network? Search your doctors, specialists and nearest hospital in that exact network.
  4. Deductible and out-of-pocket maximum. Know the most you could pay in a bad year, not just the premium.
  5. Exclusions. Read any exclusion rider attached to your approval.
  6. Prescriptions. Check each medication on the drug list.
  7. Renewal. Ask whether the policy is renewable and how rate changes are set each year.
  8. Start date and cancellation. Know when coverage begins and how to cancel without a gap.

Farm Bureau health plans are a different product

Several of our states let farm bureaus sell health plans to their members: Alabama, Arkansas, Florida, Indiana, Iowa, Kansas, Mississippi, Missouri, Nebraska, Ohio, South Dakota, Tennessee and Texas. These plans can be cheaper, but they are not insurance and are exempt from most state and federal insurance rules. They ask health questions and can decline you, and they require a farm bureau membership, typically $30 to $50 a year. If you are weighing one, we can put it next to a private PPO so you see the trade-offs.

FAQ

Private PPO health insurance: questions

What is private PPO health insurance?

Individual health insurance priced on age, location and health rather than income, usually with a PPO network you can use without referrals. Approval depends on underwriting.

Is a private PPO cheaper than an ACA plan?

For healthy people above the subsidy line it often is, because Marketplace plans are full price above 400% of the federal poverty level. If you qualify for a premium tax credit, the Marketplace may cost less.

Does a private PPO work in other states?

Many private PPO plans use national networks, so in-network care is available across the U.S. Check the specific plan's network.
