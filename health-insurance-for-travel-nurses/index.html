Health Insurance for Travel Nurses

Health insurance for travel nurses who change agencies, states and contracts: one plan that stays with you between assignments.

Reviewed by Ayoub Cherif, licensed health insurance broker (NPN 22089749) · Updated November 17, 2026

Get a Free Quote Call Us Now

Agency plans usually end when a contract ends, which leaves gaps and a new deductible every time you switch. A private PPO with a national network that you own stays the same from contract to contract, with in-network care across the U.S.

Licenses travel. Most health plans don't.

The Nurse Licensure Compact lets a nurse whose primary state of residence is a compact state hold one multistate license for every other compact state. Health insurance works differently: an individual plan is bought in the state where you live, and its network decides where you're covered. That's why travelers on agency plans often end up restarting deductibles, losing coverage between contracts or finding that the plan covers only emergencies in the next state.

A plan you own, with a national PPO network, solves all three for healthy nurses: the same plan and deductible all year, in-network care on any contract and no gap when an assignment ends. W-2 travelers generally can't take the self-employed deduction; independent 1099 nurses with a net profit usually can.

What matters for travel nurses

  • Gaps between contracts: your own plan doesn't end when an assignment does.
  • One deductible: no restarting when you change agencies mid-year.
  • W-2 vs. 1099: W-2 travelers generally can't take the self-employed deduction; independent 1099 nurses with a net profit usually can.

Between jobs or retiring early?

A gap in coverage is where people overpay most, usually on COBRA. For healthy people a private PPO usually costs less and can start any time. See health insurance between jobs.

The honest caveat: private PPO plans are medically underwritten. They can limit or exclude pre-existing conditions and approval isn't guaranteed. If someone in your household has a serious ongoing condition, a Marketplace plan can be the better choice because it can't turn you down, and we'll tell you if that's you.

Alabama: Birmingham, Huntsville and the Gulf Coast

Most Alabama travel contracts cluster around UAB Medicine in Birmingham and Huntsville Hospital in the north. Nurses based in Mobile often pick up contracts across the line in Pensacola or on the Mississippi coast, and Huntsville sits less than half an hour from Tennessee. A network that stops at the state line turns a routine visit into an out-of-network bill.

Ask any plan you're considering whether UAB Medicine, Huntsville Hospital and the Mobile-area hospitals are in network, then check Pensacola and Tennessee providers if you work near either line. If you're based in Birmingham but take most contracts elsewhere, weight the national network more heavily than the local one: you'll use it more. Keep a primary care doctor at home for checkups between assignments.

Arkansas: Little Rock, Northwest Arkansas and the borders

UAMS in Little Rock is the state's academic medical center, and Northwest Arkansas has grown fast enough to keep Washington Regional and Mercy hiring. Arkansas also shares three border metros: Texarkana with Texas, Fort Smith with Oklahoma and West Memphis with Memphis. Nurses based in any of them often work a contract on the other side.

If you live in Northwest Arkansas, confirm Washington Regional and Mercy; in central Arkansas, UAMS and Baptist Health. Then check the city across the border you're most likely to work in: Tulsa, Memphis or Texarkana. A plan that covers both your home system and those cities will handle most of your year without a single out-of-network visit.

More on Arkansas: health insurance in Arkansas

Colorado: Denver, Colorado Springs and the mountains

UCHealth, Intermountain Health and Denver Health bring travel nurses to the Front Range, and ski-season demand pulls nurses into mountain communities where the nearest large hospital can be hours away. Colorado runs its own marketplace, Connect for Health Colorado, instead of HealthCare.gov.

For Front Range nurses, confirm UCHealth, Intermountain Health and Denver Health. For winter mountain contracts, check the small hospital in that town and how the plan handles a transfer to Denver. Altitude, ski injuries and long drives make urgent care access matter; ask where the nearest in-network urgent care is before you sign a mountain contract.

More on Colorado: health insurance in Colorado

Delaware: Wilmington, Dover and the Philadelphia region

ChristianaCare is Delaware's largest health system, with Bayhealth in central Delaware and Beebe at the beaches. Wilmington is about 30 minutes from Philadelphia, so many Delaware nurses work contracts in Pennsylvania, New Jersey and Maryland. All three are compact states too, which makes the region easy to work on one license.

Confirm ChristianaCare, Bayhealth and Beebe for care at home, then the Philadelphia, South Jersey and Baltimore systems where you're likeliest to work. Because almost every Delaware traveler crosses a state line, a plan that's strong only inside Delaware is a poor fit; check the out-of-state network first.

More on Delaware: health insurance in Delaware

Georgia: Atlanta, Savannah and the state lines

Atlanta's large systems, including Emory, Piedmont and Northside, keep demand for travel nurses high. Georgia also borders three busy markets: Augusta faces Aiken across the Savannah River, Columbus faces Phenix City, Alabama, and Chattanooga's metro reaches into north Georgia. Georgia runs its own marketplace, Georgia Access.

Atlanta nurses should confirm Emory, Piedmont and Northside; Savannah and Augusta nurses should check the local systems plus South Carolina providers across the river. Many Georgia Access plans use regional networks, so compare how each plan treats Florida, the Carolinas and Tennessee before you commit to a year of contracts across the Southeast.

More on Georgia: health insurance in Georgia

Illinois: Chicago, Peoria and the Metro East

Northwestern Medicine and Rush anchor Chicago, and OSF HealthCare runs hospitals across central Illinois. Illinois nurses also live next to compact states: the Metro East is across the river from St. Louis, the Quad Cities straddle Iowa, and Northwest Indiana is part of the Chicago market. That border matters more for your license than for your health plan.

Illinois hasn't joined the Nurse Licensure Compact, so you apply by endorsement in each other state, which adds weeks before a contract can start. The health plan side is easier: buy it in Illinois with a network that reaches Missouri, Iowa and Indiana if you work across those lines.

More on Illinois: health insurance in Illinois

Indiana: Indianapolis and three border markets

IU Health, Community Health Network and Parkview Health are Indiana's big employers of nurses. Indiana also borders three metros that pull nurses across state lines: Chicago from Northwest Indiana, Louisville from Jeffersonville and Clarksville, and Owensboro and Henderson from Evansville. Kentucky is a compact state; Illinois is not.

Confirm IU Health, Community Health Network and Parkview Health at home. Northwest Indiana nurses should check Chicago providers, and Southern Indiana nurses should check Louisville. Because Indiana's compact license covers Kentucky but not Illinois, many Indiana travelers lean toward Kentucky and Ohio contracts; make sure the plan follows.

More on Indiana: health insurance in Indiana

Iowa: Des Moines, Iowa City and the river cities

University of Iowa Health Care in Iowa City is the state's academic medical center, and UnityPoint Health and MercyOne run hospitals statewide. Iowa's river cities face other states: Council Bluffs with Omaha, Sioux City with Nebraska and South Dakota, and the Quad Cities with Illinois, which isn't a compact state.

Confirm University of Iowa Health Care, UnityPoint and MercyOne. Western Iowa nurses should check Omaha's Nebraska Medicine; eastern Iowa nurses should check the Illinois side of the Quad Cities. Iowa's continuation law covers small-employer plans for nine months, which helps if you leave a small clinic job to start traveling.

More on Iowa: health insurance in Iowa

Kansas: Wichita and both sides of Kansas City

The University of Kansas Health System is the state's academic medical center, Ascension Via Christi anchors Wichita and Stormont Vail serves Topeka. The Kansas City metro runs across State Line Road into Missouri, and many nurses live on one side and work on the other.

Confirm The University of Kansas Health System and Saint Luke's on the Missouri side if you live in the Kansas City metro; Ascension Via Christi if you're in Wichita; Stormont Vail in Topeka. Western Kansas contracts can be hours from a large hospital, so check how transfers are covered.

Kentucky: Louisville, Lexington and Northern Kentucky

UK HealthCare in Lexington and Norton Healthcare and UofL Health in Louisville are the state's largest nurse employers, with Baptist Health Kentucky across the state. Covington and Northern Kentucky are part of the Cincinnati metro, Louisville reaches into Southern Indiana and Bowling Green is about an hour from Nashville. Kentucky runs its own marketplace, kynect.

Confirm UK HealthCare, Norton Healthcare, UofL Health and Baptist Health Kentucky. Northern Kentucky nurses should check Cincinnati systems, Louisville nurses Southern Indiana, and Bowling Green nurses Nashville. kynect plans are often regional; compare out-of-state coverage before you choose.

Louisiana: New Orleans, Baton Rouge and Shreveport

Ochsner Health and LCMC Health anchor south Louisiana, and Willis-Knighton is the large system in Shreveport. Shreveport–Bossier sits near East Texas, and Lake Charles is about an hour from Beaumont, so contracts in Texas are common for nurses in western Louisiana.

Confirm Ochsner and LCMC in the south and Willis-Knighton in Shreveport. Western Louisiana nurses should weigh Houston and Beaumont coverage heavily, because that's where many contracts are. Louisiana's continuation law allows 12 months for small-employer plans if you leave a staff job at a small facility.

Maryland: Baltimore, Bethesda and Washington, D.C.

Johns Hopkins, the University of Maryland Medical System and MedStar Health make Maryland one of the densest hospital markets in the country. Many Maryland nurses also take contracts in Washington, D.C., and D.C. isn't a compact jurisdiction yet, so a D.C. contract needs a D.C. license. Maryland runs its own marketplace, Maryland Health Connection.

Confirm Johns Hopkins, the University of Maryland Medical System and MedStar Health, then check D.C., Northern Virginia and Delaware providers. Maryland Health Connection plans vary in how they treat out-of-state care, so compare carefully if you plan to work outside the state.

More on Maryland: health insurance in Maryland

Michigan: Detroit, Grand Rapids and Ann Arbor

Michigan Medicine, Corewell Health and Henry Ford Health are the state's largest systems. Michigan isn't part of the Nurse Licensure Compact yet: legislation is pending, so for now a Michigan resident holds a single-state license. A contract in Toledo or Indiana needs a license in that state too.

Wisconsin contracts need a separate license as well. Your Michigan health plan is the easier part: choose one whose network follows you to Ohio, Indiana and Wisconsin.

More on Michigan: health insurance in Michigan

Mississippi: Jackson, the Gulf Coast and DeSoto County

The University of Mississippi Medical Center in Jackson is the state's academic medical center, with Baptist Memorial and Singing River Health System elsewhere. DeSoto County is part of the Memphis metro, and the Gulf Coast sits between Mobile and New Orleans, so many Mississippi nurses work contracts in Tennessee, Alabama or Louisiana.

Confirm UMMC, Baptist Memorial and Singing River Health System. DeSoto County nurses should check Memphis systems, and Gulf Coast nurses should check New Orleans and Mobile. Mississippi's continuation law covers small-employer plans for 12 months.

More on Mississippi: health insurance in Mississippi

Missouri: St. Louis, Kansas City and the Ozarks

BJC HealthCare and Mercy anchor St. Louis, Saint Luke's serves Kansas City and CoxHealth serves Springfield and the Ozarks. Missouri's two big metros both cross state lines: Kansas City into Kansas, a compact state, and St. Louis into Illinois, which isn't one.

Confirm BJC HealthCare, Mercy, CoxHealth and Saint Luke's. Kansas City nurses should check the Kansas side; St. Louis nurses should check the Illinois side, especially if they get an Illinois license for Metro East contracts.

Montana: Billings, Missoula and Bozeman

Billings Clinic, Logan Health in Kalispell and Bozeman Health are the larger systems, and much of Montana relies on small critical access hospitals far apart from each other. A contract in a rural town can mean the nearest large hospital is a long drive or a flight away.

Confirm Billings Clinic, Logan Health and Bozeman Health, and ask how the plan handles air or ground transport from a rural hospital. Montana has no general small-employer continuation law, so if you leave a small clinic job to travel, line up your own plan first.

University Medical Center of Southern Nevada in Las Vegas and Renown Health in Reno are the state's largest systems. Nevada isn't part of the Nurse Licensure Compact, and it borders California, which isn't either, and Arizona, which is. A Nevada resident needs a separate license for each state they work in.

Build that licensing time into any contract you accept. You still buy your health plan in Nevada; pick one whose network reaches the states you work in.

FAQ

Travel Nurses: questions

Should travel nurses buy their own health insurance?

Many do, because agency coverage often ends between contracts. Your own PPO with a national network keeps one plan and one deductible across assignments.

Do travel nurses lose health insurance between contracts?

Often, if coverage comes through the agency. COBRA is usually expensive, so many travelers keep their own private plan instead.

Does my compact license mean my health plan works in other states?

No. The license and the plan are separate. Your plan is bought where you live, and only a plan with a national network keeps routine care in network on out-of-state contracts.

Should I waive my agency's health insurance?

Compare first: the agency plan's cost per paycheck, deductible and network against a plan you own. Many healthy travelers keep their own PPO and take the higher pay some agencies offer when benefits are waived.
