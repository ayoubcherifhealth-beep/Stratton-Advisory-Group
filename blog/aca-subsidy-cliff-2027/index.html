The ACA Subsidy Cliff in 2027: What It Costs and How to Plan Around It

Earn one dollar over 400% of the poverty level and your premium tax credit disappears. Here are the 2027 limits, what crossing them costs, and the legal ways to stay under.

Reviewed by Ayoub Cherif, licensed health insurance broker (NPN 22089749) · November 13, 2026 · Last updated November 13, 2026

Short answer: For 2027 coverage, ACA premium tax credits stop completely once household income (MAGI) goes above 400% of the federal poverty level: $63,840 for one person, $86,560 for two, $132,000 for a family of four. One dollar over can cost an older couple more than $15,000 a year. Lowering MAGI with pre-tax retirement and HSA contributions is the main way to stay under.

Key takeaways
  • The enhanced subsidies that removed the income cap expired at the end of 2025. Congress hasn't restored them, so the 400% cliff applies to 2027 coverage.
  • Between 300% and 400% of the poverty level you pay 10.22% of income for the benchmark silver plan. Above 400% you pay the full price.
  • The cliff hurts most for people in their 50s and early 60s and in high-premium areas. For many young, single buyers it is small.
  • Starting with 2026 coverage there is no cap on paying back excess advance credits. If your income ends up over the line, you repay all of it at tax time.
  • 401(k), traditional IRA, HSA, SEP and solo 401(k) contributions lower MAGI. Roth withdrawals and savings don't raise it. Social Security and tax-exempt interest still count.
  • If you're healthy and can't get under the line, compare full-price Marketplace plans with private, medically underwritten PPOs. If you have health conditions, the Marketplace is usually the safer choice.
Ayoub’s take

Most people near the line don't need a new plan. They need a better income estimate and a plan for December. Run the numbers with your CPA in the fall, not in April when it's too late to change them.

What is the ACA subsidy cliff?

The subsidy cliff is the income level where the premium tax credit, the ACA's main subsidy, drops from a meaningful amount to zero all at once. It doesn't phase out gradually. Under the law as it stands for 2027, you qualify if your household's modified adjusted gross income (MAGI) is between 100% and 400% of the federal poverty level (FPL). Go one dollar above 400% and you lose the whole credit.

From 2021 through 2025 the cliff didn't exist. The American Rescue Plan, later extended by the Inflation Reduction Act, capped benchmark premiums at 8.5% of income no matter how much you earned. That enhancement expired on December 31, 2025. The House passed a three-year extension in January 2026, but the Senate didn't take it up, so the original rules came back. The IRS's 2027 percentage table (Rev. Proc. 2026-26) again stops at 400% of the poverty level.

How the 2027 premium tax credit is calculated

The credit fills the gap between two numbers:

  1. The benchmark premium: the full price of the second-lowest-cost silver plan in your area for the people in your household. It depends on age, ZIP code and household makeup.
  2. Your expected contribution: a percentage of your MAGI that rises with income.

Credit = benchmark premium − expected contribution (never below zero). You can put the credit toward any metal level. With a cheaper plan you pay less, and with a more expensive one you pay the difference.

So the cliff isn't a fixed dollar amount. It equals whatever credit you had just below the line, and that credit is biggest when the benchmark premium is high compared with your income. Three groups are hit hardest:

  • People aged 50 to 64. ACA plans can charge a 64-year-old up to three times what they charge a 21-year-old.
  • High-premium areas. Benchmark premiums differ a lot from county to county. KFF reports benchmark silver premiums rose 42% on average between 2025 and 2027, which makes the cliff deeper everywhere.
  • Larger households just over the line. More people means a bigger benchmark premium to lose.

Real-world data points the same way. KFF estimated that a 60-year-old earning $65,000 (about 415% of the poverty level) would pay $10,389 more for 2026 coverage once the enhanced credits expired. Healthinsurance.org's 2027 example of a 63-year-old couple in West Virginia shows benchmark-level premiums taking a large share of income just above the line.

What counts as income: MAGI explained

The cliff is based on modified adjusted gross income (MAGI) for everyone in your tax household, not on your salary or take-home pay. For the premium tax credit, MAGI is:

  • your adjusted gross income (AGI, line 11 of Form 1040), plus
  • tax-exempt interest, such as municipal bond interest, plus
  • the non-taxable part of Social Security benefits, plus
  • excluded foreign earned income.

Your tax household is you, your spouse if you file jointly, and anyone you claim as a dependent. A dependent's income counts only if they're required to file a tax return. Married couples generally must file jointly to claim the credit; there are limited exceptions, for example for victims of domestic abuse or spousal abandonment.

Worked example (hypothetical). The 62-year-old couple above expects a MAGI of $95,000, which is $8,440 over the $86,560 line. If one spouse still works, they could add $8,440 or more in pre-tax 401(k) deferrals before December 31. If both are retired, they could put $9,000 + $1,000 + $1,000 = $11,000 into a family HSA (on an HSA-eligible plan, with $1,000 catch-ups for each spouse 55+, each in their own HSA) and fund part of the year's spending from cash instead of IRA withdrawals. Either way they're under the line, and using the same hypothetical $24,000 benchmark, the credit is worth about $15,000. That money stays in their accounts rather than going to taxes or premiums.

A rule of thumb: reducing income only makes sense when the move is something you'd be glad to do anyway (saving for retirement, funding an HSA, deferring a sale) or when the credit you keep is clearly worth more than what the move costs you. Don't turn down real income just to keep a credit worth less than the income.

Early retirees: plan the bridge years

Retiring before 65 is when the cliff matters most, because your premiums are high and you have the most control over your reported income. Common approaches, worked out with a tax professional, include:

  • covering living costs from cash and taxable accounts (only gains count, not the cost basis)
  • keeping Roth conversions to the room left under the line, or doing larger conversions in alternate years and accepting full-price premiums in those years
  • thinking about when to claim Social Security, since the full benefit counts toward MAGI.

Our early retirement health insurance guide covers the coverage side of the bridge to Medicare.

Self-employed and business owners

Net profit counts, not gross receipts. That gives owners more control than employees have: a solo 401(k) or SEP contribution, an HSA, timing of equipment purchases and invoices, and the self-employed health insurance deduction all reduce MAGI. Several of these can be made after December 31 and still count for that tax year, which helps when you only know your final numbers in the spring. See health insurance for the self-employed for the coverage options.

If your income ends up higher: full repayment, no cap

Most people get the credit in advance: the Marketplace sends it to the insurer every month based on your estimated income. At tax time, Form 8962 compares that estimate with your actual MAGI.

  • Starting with tax year 2026, the IRS says you must repay the full amount of any excess advance credit. The old repayment caps, which used to limit what lower- and middle-income households owed, were removed by the 2025 federal tax law.
  • If your final MAGI is above 400% of the poverty level, you have to repay every dollar of the advance credit you received that year.

Hypothetical example: A 62-year-old couple estimates $80,000 and receives about $15,800 in advance credits over the year (hypothetical $24,000 benchmark). A consulting project pushes their final MAGI to $90,000. They repay the whole $15,800 with their 2027 tax return, unless they can bring MAGI back under $86,560 before filing with IRA, HSA or SEP contributions.

How to avoid a surprise bill:

  • Update your income on HealthCare.gov or your state exchange when it changes. Don't wait for renewal.
  • If your income is uncertain, take less of the credit in advance and claim the rest on your tax return.
  • Check your year-to-date income in October and plan any December contributions then.

Other 2027 rules that affect people near the line

  • Open enrollment on HealthCare.gov runs from November 1, 2026 to January 15, 2027. Pick a plan by December 15 for coverage starting January 1. A rule that would have shortened open enrollment was vacated in court. Some state exchanges set their own dates. See open enrollment 2027 dates.
  • Out-of-pocket maximum: up to $12,000 for one person and $24,000 for a family in 2027 (in-network). Budget for a bad year, not just the premium.
  • HSA-eligible bronze and catastrophic plans: since January 1, 2026, Marketplace bronze and catastrophic plans count as HSA-compatible. That makes the HSA strategy available to more people near the cliff.
  • Cost-sharing reductions (lower deductibles on silver plans) only apply up to 250% of the poverty level, so they don't affect most people near the cliff.
  • Some lawfully present immigrants lose eligibility for the credit starting January 1, 2027. Check HealthCare.gov if this applies to you.
  • The low end: below 100% of the poverty level there is generally no credit. In states that expanded Medicaid, adults up to 138% usually qualify for Medicaid instead. Of the 31 states we serve, Alabama, Florida, Georgia, Kansas, Mississippi, South Carolina, Tennessee, Texas, Wisconsin and Wyoming have not expanded Medicaid.

Common mistakes

  • Using gross pay or salary instead of MAGI. Pre-tax deductions lower it, and Social Security and muni interest raise it.
  • Forgetting a dependent's income, when that dependent is required to file a tax return.
  • Leaving Social Security out. The whole benefit counts, including the untaxed part.
  • Doing a Roth conversion in December without checking how much room is left under the line.
  • Assuming the old repayment caps still apply. They ended with tax year 2026.
  • Not updating the Marketplace after a raise, a new client or a home sale.
  • Lowering income when the credit is small anyway. Younger single buyers may save very little.
  • Comparing premiums only. Compare the year's total cost: premiums plus the deductible and out-of-pocket maximum.

Who this guide is for, and who it isn't for

For: households with 2027 MAGI between about 300% and 450% of the poverty level, early retirees under 65, the self-employed and business owners with variable income, couples aged 50 to 64, and anyone who got a large credit in 2025 and is now paying full price.

Not for: people offered employer coverage the IRS treats as affordable (in 2027, the employee pays no more than 10.22% of household income for it), people on Medicare, and households far above the line, for whom a few thousand dollars of contributions won't change the result. For them the question is which full-price or private plan gives the best value.

What to do next

  1. Estimate your 2027 MAGI. Start with last year's AGI and adjust for expected changes, Social Security, tax-exempt interest and planned Roth conversions.
  2. Find your line. Use the table above or our subsidy calculator.
  3. Price the cliff. On HealthCare.gov or your state exchange, compare the price with and without the credit. If it's small, stop here.
  4. Build your MAGI plan with your CPA. Use the levers above. Note which can be done after December 31.
  5. Choose a plan before December 15 for a January 1 start. If you'll be over the line, get quotes on a private PPO as well if you're healthy.
  6. Check again in October 2027. Update the Marketplace and use December or filing-season contributions to land where you planned.

FAQ

ACA subsidy cliff questions

What is the ACA subsidy cliff in 2027?

It's the income point where the ACA premium tax credit drops to zero all at once. For 2027 coverage, households with MAGI above 400% of the federal poverty level get no credit and pay the full premium, because the enhanced credits that removed the cap expired at the end of 2025.

What is the income limit for ACA subsidies in 2027?

400% of the federal poverty level: $63,840 for one person, $86,560 for two, $109,280 for three and $132,000 for a family of four in the 48 contiguous states and DC. Alaska and Hawaii have higher limits.

Did Congress extend the enhanced ACA subsidies for 2027?

No. The House passed a three-year extension in January 2026, but it didn't become law. The IRS's 2027 tables use the original rules, with credits ending at 400% of the poverty level.

How much does it cost to go over the subsidy cliff?

You lose whatever credit you had just below the line. For older buyers in high-premium areas that can be $10,000 to $15,000 or more a year. For young single buyers it can be close to nothing. Compare your price with and without the credit on HealthCare.gov.

Do I have to pay back my ACA subsidy if I earn more than expected?

Yes. Starting with tax year 2026 there's no repayment cap. If your final MAGI is above 400% of the poverty level, you repay all the advance credit you received for that year when you file your taxes.

Does Social Security count toward the ACA subsidy limit?

Yes. MAGI for the premium tax credit includes all your Social Security benefits, including the part that isn't taxed. Tax-exempt interest also counts.

Can 401(k) or HSA contributions get me under the subsidy cliff?

Yes. Pre-tax 401(k) deferrals (up to $25,500 in 2027, more with catch-ups) and deductible HSA contributions ($4,500 self-only or $9,000 family) lower MAGI. IRA, HSA, SEP and solo 401(k) employer contributions can usually be made up to your tax filing deadline.

Does a Roth conversion count as income for ACA subsidies?

Yes. The converted amount is added to your AGI and MAGI for that year. Qualified withdrawals from a Roth IRA don't count, which is why early retirees often keep conversions to the room left under the line.

What are my options if I'm over the cliff?

Buy a full-price Marketplace or off-exchange ACA plan, use a spouse's employer plan, or, if you're healthy, compare a private, medically underwritten PPO priced on your health rather than your income. Private plans ask health questions and can decline you, so people with conditions are usually better off on an ACA plan.
