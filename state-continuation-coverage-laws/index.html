State Continuation (Mini-COBRA) Laws in 31 States

Federal COBRA covers employers with 20 or more employees. For smaller employers, state law decides whether you can keep your plan, for how long and at what price.

Reviewed by Ayoub Cherif, licensed health insurance broker (NPN 22089749) · Updated November 15, 2026

Get a Free Quote Call Us Now

26 of the 31 states we're licensed in have a state continuation law for small employers; Alabama, Indiana, Michigan, Montana and Nevada don't have a general one. These laws generally apply to fully insured plans of employers with fewer than 20 employees, and the person continuing coverage pays the full premium.

Summary based on Mercer's November 2024 review of state continuation laws, with the statute for each state. State laws change, election deadlines vary and are often shorter than federal COBRA's 60 days, and self-funded plans generally aren't subject to state law. Your election notice and the carrier confirm what applies to you.

The cheaper route for healthy people

Whether it's federal COBRA or a state law, you pay the whole premium your employer used to share. Healthy people usually find a private PPO with a national network costs less and doesn't end after a set number of months. Use the election window to apply first, and elect continuation only if you need it.

One honest caveat: private PPO plans are medically underwritten. A pre-existing condition can be excluded or lead to a decline. If that's a concern for anyone in your family, we'll compare a the Marketplace plan alongside it and say plainly if it's the better fit.

FAQ

Mini-COBRA laws: questions

What is mini-COBRA?

A state law that lets employees of smaller employers, usually fewer than 20 employees, keep their group health plan for a period after losing it, generally at the full premium.

Which states have no mini-COBRA law?

Among the 31 states we serve: Alabama, Indiana, Michigan, Montana and Nevada have no general state continuation law for small employers.

Is mini-COBRA cheaper than COBRA?

Not usually. Both charge the full group premium; some states allow a small fee on top, and Florida allows up to 115%.
